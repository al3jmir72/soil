# Chapter 1 — Geotechnical Engineering: A Historical Perspective

> Study notes (my own condensed summary) of Ch. 1 in Das & Sobhan, *Principles of Geotechnical Engineering*, 9th ed., Cengage (2018), pp. 1–15.
> This chapter is mostly history — focus on **who contributed what** and the **four periods**, which are typical exam/quiz material.

---

## 1. Key definitions (Sec. 1.1)

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Soil** (engineering sense) | Uncemented aggregate of mineral grains and decayed organic matter (the solid particles) with liquid and gas in the voids between them |
| **Soil mechanics** | Science of the physical properties of soil and the behaviour of soil masses under forces |
| **Soils engineering** | Applying soil-mechanics principles to practical problems |
| **Geotechnical engineering** | Civil-engineering sub-discipline dealing with natural materials near the earth's surface: soil mechanics + rock mechanics applied to **foundations, retaining structures and earth structures** |

Soil is both a **construction material** and a **foundation material**, so engineers need to know its origin, grain-size distribution, drainage, compressibility, strength, and ability to support loads and resist deformation.

---

## 2. Before the 18th century (Sec. 1.2)

- Early geotechnical work was **experience-based (trial and error)**, not scientific. Some structures survived, others crumbled.
- Ancient civilisations grew along rivers: Nile, Tigris–Euphrates, Yellow River (Huang Ho), Indus.
  - Dykes on the Indus (~2000 B.C., Mohenjo-Daro) and in China for irrigation; no evidence of foundation stabilisation or erosion control.
  - Greeks used isolated pad footings, strip footings and rafts.
  - Egyptian pyramids (from ~2700 B.C.; 138 found as of 2008) raised foundation, slope-stability and underground-chamber challenges (Table 1.1 lists the major ones: Djoser, Sneferu ×3, Khufu, Djedefre, Khafre, Menkaure).
  - Chinese pagodas (after Buddhism arrived in 68 A.D.) were often built on silt/soft clay; foundation pressures sometimes exceeded bearing capacity → damage.

### Famous bearing-capacity/settlement problems

| Structure | Facts |
|---|---|
| **Leaning Tower of Pisa** (begun 1173 A.D., built in stages over 200+ years) | ≈ 15,700 t; circular base ≈ 20 m diameter; a **weak clay layer ~11 m deep** compressed unevenly → tilt. Over 5 m out of plumb on a 54 m height (≈ 5.5°). Closed 1990; stabilised by extracting ≈ 70 t of soil from under the **north** side in 41 extractions → tilt reduced to ≈ 5°. |
| **Garisenda Tower** (Bologna, 12th c.) | 48 m high, ≈ 4,210 t, tilt ≈ 4° |
| **Asinelli Tower** (Bologna) | 97 m high, ≈ 7,300 t, tilt ≈ 1.3° |

## 3. Timeline of the four periods (Skempton, 1985)

| Period | Years | Theme |
|---|---|---|
| **Preclassical** | 1700 – 1776 | Natural slopes, unit weights, semi-empirical earth-pressure theories |
| **Classical soil mechanics – Phase I** | 1776 – 1856 | Rigorous earth-pressure theory (mostly French) |
| **Classical soil mechanics – Phase II** | 1856 – 1910 | Lab tests on sand; permeability, stress distribution, dilatancy |
| **Modern soil mechanics** | 1910 – 1927 | Fundamental behaviour of **clays** established |

### 3.1 Preclassical (1700–1776)
- **Henri Gautier (1717):** studied natural slopes of soil heaps (= angle of repose) for retaining-wall design. Clean dry sand ≈ 31°, ordinary earth ≈ 45°. Recommended unit weights: dry sand 18.1 kN/m³ (115 lb/ft³); ordinary earth 13.4 kN/m³ (85 lb/ft³).
- **Bernard Forest de Belidor (1729):** textbook for military/civil engineers; lateral earth-pressure theory for retaining walls; early soil classification with unit weights (e.g., firm sand 16.7–18.4 kN/m³; ordinary earth 13.4; soft earth/silt 16.0; clay 18.9).
- **Francois Gadroy (1746):** first lab model test — a 76 mm high sand-backfill wall; observed **slip planes** at failure (summarised by Mayniel, 1808).
- **Jean Perronet (~1769):** slope stability; distinguished intact ground from fills.

### 3.2 Classical Phase I (1776–1856)
- **Charles Coulomb (1776):** used calculus (maxima/minima) to find the true sliding surface behind a retaining wall, applying **friction and cohesion** laws.
- **de Prony (1790):** included Coulomb's theory in his textbook *Nouvelle Architecture Hydraulique*.
- **Francais (1820) & Navier:** special cases — inclined backfills, surcharge.
- **Jean Poncelet (1840):** **graphical** solution for earth pressure on vertical/inclined walls with irregular ground; first to use the symbol **φ** for soil friction angle; gave the first **ultimate bearing-capacity theory** for shallow foundations.
- **Alexandre Collin (1846):** deep slip surfaces in clay slopes/cuts/embankments; failure when mobilised cohesion exceeds available cohesion; observed failure surfaces ≈ **cycloid arcs**.
- **William Rankine (1857)**: simplified Coulomb's theory → ends Phase I.

### 3.3 Classical Phase II (1856–1910)
- **Henri Darcy (1856):** permeability of sand filters → **coefficient of permeability (hydraulic conductivity)**.
- **George Darwin:** lab tests of overturning moment on a hinged wall retaining loose/dense sand.
- **Joseph Boussinesq (1885):** stress distribution under loaded areas in a homogeneous, semi-infinite, elastic, isotropic medium.
- **Osborne Reynolds (1887):** **dilatancy** in sand.
- **Clibborn & Beresford:** seepage and uplift pressure (e.g., Narora Weir on the Ganges, 1898).

### 3.4 Modern period (1910–1927)
- **Albert Atterberg (~1908–1911):** clay-size fraction (< 2 µm by weight); consistency of cohesive soils — **liquid, plastic, shrinkage limits**; plasticity index = LL − PL.
- **Jean Fontard (1914):** investigated the 1909 failure of the 17 m **Charmes earth dam** (France); undrained double-shear tests on clay.
- **Arthur Bell (1915):** lateral pressure/resistance in clay and shallow-foundation bearing capacity in clay; shear-box tests for undrained strength.
- **Wolmar Fellenius (1918, 1926):** stability of saturated clay slopes (**φ = 0** analysis) assuming **circular** slip surfaces.
- **Karl Terzaghi (1919–1924 research; 1925 book):** **consolidation theory** (done at the American Robert College, Istanbul, using five clays, LL 36–67, PI 18–38); published in *Erdbaumechanik* (1925).

## 4. Geotechnical engineering after 1927 (Sec. 1.7)

**Karl Terzaghi (1883–1963) — "father of modern soil mechanics"**
- Born in Prague; mechanical-engineering degree, Graz (1904); Doctor of Technical Sciences, Graz (1912).
- Istanbul (Imperial School of Engineers 1916; Robert College 1918–1925) → MIT visiting lecturer (1925–29) → Technical University of Vienna (1929) → Harvard (1939).
- 1939 James Forrest Lecture ("Soil Mechanics — A New Chapter in Engineering Science"): foundation failures were no longer "acts of God".

**Landmarks**

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| 1936 | **1st ISSMFE conference**, Harvard, presided by Terzaghi (organised through Arthur Casagrande's efforts); ~200 people from 21 countries. Topics: effective stress, shear strength, Dutch cone, consolidation, centrifuge testing, elastic theory/stress distribution, preloading, swelling clays, frost action, earthquakes/liquefaction, machine vibration, arching |
| 1943 | Terzaghi, *Theoretical Soil Mechanics* |
| 1948 | Terzaghi & Peck, *Soil Mechanics in Engineering Practice*; Taylor, *Fundamentals of Soil Mechanics*; journal *Géotechnique* starts; **2nd ISSMFE conference, Rotterdam** (~600 participants) — **Skempton's φ = 0 concept** for clays |
| 1954 | Skempton's pore-pressure parameters **A and B** → effective-stress analysis became practical |
| 1957 | Bishop & Henkel, *The Measurement of Soil Properties in the Triaxial Test* |
| 1950s onward | Finite-difference and **finite-element** methods (e.g., Abaqus, Plaxis, Flac) enter geotechnics; numerical modelling expected to dominate in coming decades |
| 1960 | Bishop et al. — factors controlling strength of **partially saturated** cohesive soils → growth of **unsaturated soil mechanics** |
| 1997 | ISSMFE renamed **ISSMGE** (International Society of Soil Mechanics and *Geotechnical* Engineering) |

**ISSMFE/ISSMGE conferences (Table 1.2):** I Harvard 1936 · II Rotterdam 1948 · III Zurich 1953 · IV London 1957 · V Paris 1961 · VI Montreal 1965 · VII Mexico City 1969 · VIII Moscow 1973 · IX Tokyo 1977 · X Stockholm 1981 · XI San Francisco 1985 · XII Rio de Janeiro 1989 · XIII New Delhi 1994 · XIV Hamburg 1997 · XV Istanbul 2001 · XVI Osaka 2005 · XVII Alexandria 2009 · XVIII Paris 2013 · XIX Seoul 2017.

**ISSMGE technical committees (Table 1.3, 2013):** grouped into *Fundamentals* (e.g., laboratory testing, in-situ tests, numerical methods, physical modelling, unsaturated soils), *Applications* (e.g., transportation, earthquake, tunnelling, slope stability, offshore, dams, ground improvement, deep foundations, scour, soft soils) and *Impact on Society* (e.g., environmental geotechnics, frost, historic sites, forensic, disaster mitigation, risk, megacities).

**Practical notes from Sec. 1.7**
- Soils are **heterogeneous** — properties can vary a lot within a few metres. Design parameters must come from **site investigation**: field tests, samples at various depths/locations, and lab tests.
- Tests follow **ASTM** standards (soil/rock/aggregates in volumes **04.08 and 04.09**).
- Key journals: Canadian Geotechnical Journal; J. Geotechnical and Geoenvironmental Engineering (ASCE); Geotechnical and Geological Engineering; Geotechnical Testing Journal (ASTM); Géotechnique; Int. J. Geomechanics (ASCE); Int. J. Geotechnical Engineering; Soils and Foundations.

## 5. End of an era — Ralph B. Peck (Sec. 1.8)

- Ralph B. Peck (1912–2008): worked as Terzaghi's assistant on the Chicago Subway (1939–43); professor of foundation engineering at the University of Illinois (1948–74); ISSMGE president 1969–73; National Medal of Science (1974); 250+ publications.
- Consulted on rapid-transit systems (Chicago, San Francisco, Washington, D.C.), Alaska pipeline, James Bay Project, Heathrow Express, Dead Sea dikes; final project: Rion–Antirion Bridge (Greece).
- Described as the last of the early giants (Terzaghi, Casagrande, Taylor, Skempton, Peck).
