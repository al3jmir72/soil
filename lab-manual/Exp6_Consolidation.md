# Experiment 6: Consolidation

## Introduction

When a new structure is constructed, the stress in the soil mass supporting the structure is changed. If this change in stress increases the pressure on the soil, the soil will settle beneath the applied load. If the change in soil stress results in a decrease in a soil pressure, such as when soil is removed in an excavation, soil may exhibit the opposite effect (i.e., heave). The amount of volume change that occur in the soil is often one of the governing design criteria of a project. Thus, it is important to estimate the amount of soil compression or consolidation.

## Objectives

• To conduct the consolidation test. • &emsp; To determine the compression index C<sub>c</sub> which is used to estimate the total amount of primary consolidation settlement expected to occur in a soil strata.

• To determine the coefficient of consolidation C<sub>v</sub> which is used to estimate the time rate of consolidation.

## Theoretical Background

When an additional stress (Δσ) is applied to a saturated soil specimen, the entire stress is immediately (at t = 0) carried by the pore water as excess pore pressure (Δσ = Δu). When some time (t = t<sub>0</sub>) elapses, some of the excess pore pressure is dissipated and the effective stress increases by a similar amount. As more time elapses, an additional amount of the excess pore pressure is dissipated and more of (Δσ) is transferred to the soil solids as an increase in effective stress. Finally, after a very long time (t = ∞), the increase in stress (Δσ) is completely transferred to the soil skeleton as effective stress. This leads to the reduction in the void ratio and a reduction in the sample thickness. This reduction is termed consolidation.

## Specimen Preparation

Before preparing the specimen, the porous stones should be placed in distilled water for several minutes to ensure that the stones are saturated and will not draw water from the specimen. The procedure for inserting the sample into the consolidometer differs if a remolded specimen or an undisturbed specimen is to be tested. If testing an undisturbed specimen, the specimen should be carefully trimmed to the same diameter as the consolidometer ring. The prepared specimen is inserted into the ring making sure that it flushes with both the top and the bottom of the consolidometer ring. If a remolded specimen is to be tested, soil at the proper moisture content is placed in the consolidometer ring using a spatula avoiding lumps and voids. The soil and the consolidometer ring is weighed to the nearest 0.01 g and recorded as W<sub>si</sub>.

Filter paper should be inserted between each porous stone and the soil specimen to prevent intrusion of the soil into the porous stone. The initial moisture content should be determined and recorded as w<sub>i</sub>.

## Procedure

1. Assemble the consolidometer and place it on the loading device (Figure 6.1). Place the loading cap on the porous stone.

2. Fill the grooves in the base of the consolidometer with water.

3. Install the dial gage and record the dial reading corresponding to zero load d<sub>z</sub>.

4. Apply a load increment and record the dial reading at 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1,2, 4, 8, 15 and 30 minutes and 1, 2, 4, 8 and 24 hour.

5. Increase the loading increment by approximately doubling the previous applied pressure each time until the desired pressure is obtained. Usually the loading sequence consists of 20, 40, 80, 160 and 320 kPa. The dial reading versus time is recorded for the load increment.

6. If unloading characteristics are to be measured, the specimen should be unloaded in the same order in which it was loaded taking readings in accordance with step 4.

7. When dial readings indicate no further significant rebound, remove the dial indicator and disassemble the apparatus and determine the water content of the specimen.

## Presentation of Results

Two graphical fitting methods are used to present the dial reading versus time, namely the logarithm-of-time fitting method and square-root-of time method.

## Logarithm-of-time Method

The logarithm-of-time method is applied by plotting the dial reading on the vertical axis and the logarithm of the corresponding elapsed time as the abscissa. A typical dial reading versus long-time curve is shown in Figure 6.3. The data taken during any loading increment represents two types of compression. The first type is called primary consolidation. However, even after the excess pore water pressure has been dissipated, a small amount of additional compression will occur. This additional compression is termed secondary consolidation and is believed to be a continuing soil-particle readjustment under constant effective stress. The point of division between primary and secondary consolidation is determined graphically as follows:

1. Extend the straight-line portion of the curve defined by the 8, 16 and 24 hour data points back toward the vertical axis.

2. Extend downward the straight-line portion of the center of the reverse S- shaped curve until it intersects to straight-line extension of step 1.

3. The intersection of the two straight-line extensions is considered to be the division between primary and secondary consolidation. The dial reading corresponding to this point of intersection is termed d<sub>100</sub> i.e., 100% consolidation and the corresponding time is termed t<sub>100</sub>.

When a new pressure is applied there is usually a larger initial compression attributed to the compression of entrapped air. To determine graphically the dial reading at the time the new pressure is applied the following steps are undertaken:

1. Select one of the initial data points (e.g., at 0.25, 0.5 or 1 min.) and call it t<sub>1</sub>.

2. Multiply t<sub>1</sub> by 4 to obtain t<sub>2</sub>.

3. The difference in dial readings corresponding to t<sub>1</sub> and t<sub>2</sub> is y.

4. The point d<sub>0</sub> is found by laying off a distance y above t<sub>1</sub> data point.

The dial gage reading corresponding to 50% consolidation is obtained as d<sub>50</sub> = (d<sub>0</sub>+d<sub>100</sub>)/2.

## Square-root-of-time Method

In the square-root-of-time method, the dial reading is plotted as the ordinate and the square root of the elapsed time as the abscissa (Figure 6.4).

1. The zero point, d<sub>0</sub>, is obtained by extending the straight-line portion of the curve upward until it intersects the vertical axis (zero time).

2. The point of 90% consolidation is obtained by drawing another straight line through d<sub>0</sub> that has an inverse slope of 1.15 times that line obtained in step 1.

3. The point where the line obtained in step 2 intersects the curve is defined as the point of 90% consolidation d<sub>90</sub>. To obtain d<sub>100</sub>: d<sub>100</sub> = d<sub>0</sub> −10/9(d<sub>0</sub> − d<sub>90</sub>)

## Void Ratio–log Pressure Curve

The void ratio-log pressure (e-log P) curve is obtained by plotting the void ratio corresponding to the last dial reading of the load increment as the ordinate, against the logarithm of the total applied pressure as the abscissa (Figure 6.5).

e-log p curve

![Figure (PDF p. 68)](images/p068_1.png)

Figure 6.5. e-log p curve

The compression index, C<sub>c</sub>, is found as the slope of the straight-line portion of the e- versus log-P curve, and is given as:

C<sub>c</sub> =(e<sub>1</sub> − e<sub>2</sub>)/(log P<sub>2</sub> − log P<sub>1</sub>)=(e<sub>1</sub> − e<sub>2</sub>)/(log (P<sub>2</sub>/P<sub>1</sub>)) where, P<sub>1</sub> and P<sub>2</sub> are selected pressure from the straight line portion of the curve and e<sub>1</sub> and e<sub>2</sub> are the corresponding void ratios.

The swell index, C<sub>s</sub> is found as the slope of the straight line portion of the rebound curve for the unloading stage.

## Calculations

1. Specimen height at end of each loading increment

H<sub>i</sub> = H<sub>0</sub> – (d<sub>fi</sub> – d<sub>z</sub>) where, H<sub>0</sub> = original specimen height d<sub>fi</sub> = dial reading at end of each load increment.

2. Height of solids H<sub>s</sub> =W<sub>s</sub>/(G<sub>s</sub> γ<sub>w</sub> A) where, A = area of specimen

G<sub>s</sub> = specific gravity of solids γ<sub>w</sub> = unit weight of water

3. Void ratio before test e<sub>0</sub> e<sub>0</sub> =(H<sub>0</sub> − H<sub>s</sub>)/H<sub>s</sub>

4. Void ratio at end of test e<sub>f</sub> e<sub>f</sub> =(H<sub>f</sub> − H<sub>s</sub>)/H<sub>s</sub>

where, H<sub>f</sub> = height of specimen at end of test

H<sub>f</sub> = H<sub>0</sub> − ΔH where, ΔH is the net change in height of specimen.

E. Void Ratio e

The change in thickness of the specimen, ΔH, during the 500 Ib/ft<sup>2</sup> loading can be computed by subtracting the initial dial reading (0) from the reading representing 100% primary consolidation of the loading (0.0158). Hence, the change in thickness (ΔH) is 0.0158 in., or 0.0401 cm. The change in void ratio, Δe, can be computed by dividing change in thickness (ΔH) by height of solid in the specimen (H<sub>s</sub>). The height of solid in the specimen was determined previously (by dividing volume of solid in the specimen by area of the specimen) to be 0.881 cm. Hence, Δe =ΔH/H<sub>s</sub>=0.0401/0.881 = 0.046 Finally, the void ratio e for the 500 Ib/ft<sup>2</sup> loading can be determined by subtracting the change in void ratio Δe from the initial void ratio e0. Hence, e = e0 −Δe = 1.249 – 0.046 = 1.203
